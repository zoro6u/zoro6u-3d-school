# Biology lab facts (ground truth for the teacher)

## The animal cell
- Animal cells are eukaryotic: they have a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles. A typical animal cell is roughly 10–30 micrometres across.
- The 3D model is schematic and not to scale. Real organelles are smaller and the cell is much more crowded.
- Animal cells have no cell wall, no chloroplasts and no large central vacuole. Those are features of plant cells.

## Organelles in the lab
- Nucleus: stores the cell's DNA as chromosomes and controls the cell by regulating which genes are used. It is surrounded by a double membrane (nuclear envelope) with pores that let mRNA out. The nucleolus inside makes parts of ribosomes. Almost every human cell has one nucleus; mature red blood cells have none.
- Mitochondria: carry out most of aerobic respiration and make most of the cell's ATP. They have a double membrane; the inner membrane is folded into cristae, which increases surface area. They have their own small circular DNA, which in humans is inherited almost entirely from the mother. The widely accepted endosymbiotic theory says mitochondria descend from bacteria that were engulfed by an ancestral cell. Overall respiration: glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water + energy (ATP).
- Ribosomes: build proteins by reading mRNA and joining amino acids in order. They are made of rRNA and protein and have no membrane. Some are free in the cytoplasm and some are attached to the rough ER. A ribosome adds only a few amino acids per second.
- Rough endoplasmic reticulum (rough ER): a network of flattened, folded sacs studded with ribosomes. Proteins made there are threaded into it, folded, and sent on, often to the Golgi apparatus, for the cell membrane or for export. It is continuous with the nuclear envelope. Cells that export lots of protein (such as antibody-producing white blood cells) have a lot of rough ER.
- Smooth endoplasmic reticulum (smooth ER): tubes with no ribosomes. It makes lipids (phospholipids and steroid hormones), helps detoxify drugs and alcohol (very active in liver cells), and in muscle cells stores calcium.
- Golgi apparatus: a stack of flattened sacs that modifies, sorts and packages proteins and lipids from the ER into vesicles, like a post office. Named after Camillo Golgi, who described it in 1898.
- Lysosomes: small sacs of digestive enzymes that break down worn-out organelles, food particles and germs. Their inside is acidic, around pH 4.5–5, while the cytosol is close to neutral (about pH 7.2).
- Cell membrane: a double layer of phospholipids with proteins. It is selectively permeable, controlling what enters and leaves, and lets the cell receive signals. It is only about 7–10 nanometres thick.
- Cytoplasm: everything inside the membrane except the nucleus. The jelly-like fluid part is the cytosol, mostly water with dissolved salts and molecules. Many reactions happen here, for example glycolysis, the first steps of respiration. The cytoskeleton, a network of protein fibres, gives the cell shape and lets organelles move.

## Protein pathway (a good story to tell)
- DNA in the nucleus is copied into mRNA → mRNA leaves through a nuclear pore → a ribosome (often on the rough ER) builds the protein → the protein is folded in the ER → the Golgi modifies and packs it into a vesicle → the vesicle delivers it to the membrane or outside the cell.

## How the lab works
- The student can orbit and zoom the cell, click an organelle or choose it from the list, and the camera flies to it and shows its function.
- The Cutaway toggle slices a wedge out of the membrane and cytoplasm so the inside is visible; the organelles are not cut.
