# Physics lab facts (ground truth for the teacher)

## Simple pendulum
- A simple pendulum is a small mass (the bob) on a light rod or string of length L, swinging under gravity g. The lab simulates the full nonlinear equation: angular acceleration = −(g/L)·sin θ − damping term. It does not use the small-angle shortcut.
- Small-angle period: T = 2π√(L/g). It is accurate when the starting angle is small, below about 15° (error under 0.5%).
- For larger angles the real period is longer than 2π√(L/g): about 1.7% longer at 30°, 7.3% at 60°, 18% at 90°. The lab shows the exact period next to the small-angle one and says so when the angle is above 15°.
- The period does NOT depend on the mass of the bob. Mass cancels out of the equation of motion. It depends on length and gravity.
- Period grows with the square root of length: making the pendulum 4 times longer doubles the period.
- Gravity presets in the lab: Earth 9.81 m/s², Moon 1.62 m/s², Mars 3.71 m/s². A given pendulum swings about 2.46 times slower on the Moon than on Earth, and about 1.63 times slower on Mars.
- A 1.00 m pendulum on Earth has a small-angle period of about 2.006 s.
- Energy: kinetic energy KE = ½·m·L²·ω² and potential energy PE = m·g·L·(1 − cos θ), measured from the lowest point. At the highest points the pendulum is momentarily at rest and all energy is potential. At the bottom all of it is kinetic.
- With no damping the total energy stays constant. With damping, energy is turned into heat, the amplitude shrinks, and the period becomes very slightly longer.
- Christiaan Huygens built the first pendulum clock in 1656.

## Mass–spring system
- Hooke's law for an ideal spring: F = −k·x. The restoring force is proportional to the stretch and points back toward equilibrium. k is the spring constant in N/m: a stiffer spring has a bigger k.
- Period: T = 2π√(m/k). It does not depend on the amplitude (how far you pull) for an ideal spring, and in the lab's horizontal setup gravity does not matter.
- A heavier block gives a longer period; a stiffer spring gives a shorter period. 4 times the mass doubles the period.
- Energy: KE = ½·m·v², spring PE = ½·k·x². Total energy = ½·k·A² where A is the amplitude. Without damping it is constant.

## Simple harmonic motion
- Motion where the restoring force is proportional to displacement is called simple harmonic motion (SHM). The ideal spring is exactly SHM. The pendulum is approximately SHM at small angles.

## How the lab works
- The lab has two experiments: Pendulum and Mass–spring. Charts show angle (or displacement) versus time, and kinetic, potential and total energy versus time.
- The "measured period" is found from the simulation itself by timing successive upward zero-crossings of the angle or displacement, so it is a real measurement, not a formula.
- Changing damping continues the motion. Changing any other parameter restarts the run, because the physical system has changed.
- Control ranges: pendulum length 0.2–2.5 m, mass 0.1–5 kg, starting angle 5–170°, damping 0–0.5 N·m·s. Spring constant 1–100 N/m, block mass 0.1–5 kg, starting stretch 0.05–0.5 m, damping 0–2 N·s/m.

## Common misconceptions
- "Heavier pendulums swing faster": false, the period is independent of mass.
- "A bigger swing takes the same time": only approximately, and only for small angles.
